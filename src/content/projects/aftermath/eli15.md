Software has a place to try things before they go live. A new button can sit on a staging server. A new pricing rule, for a real business, usually does not. The idea goes straight into production, and the first people who feel it are actual customers.

Aftermath is a staging environment for those decisions. It builds a small fake world of customers, lets them live through a proposed change, and then tells you what happened — including the ugly chain reactions that a slide deck never shows.

Imagine a support company in Bengaluru. People file tickets. An assistant tries to help. Some tickets escalate. Finance approves or rejects a discount. Someone gets so tired they leave. Aftermath invents those people. Each one has a plan, a history, a temper, a memory, and a point at which they walk away. They are not answering a survey. They are going through the month.

You propose a change: raise prices, turn off phone routing, tweak the assistant. The world engine then steps through thirty days. One event can cause another. A price increase becomes a complaint. The assistant mishandles billing. The ticket escalates. A discount is offered. Finance says no. The customer churns. That whole chain is the point.

When the simulated month hits the breaking day, the room can be interrupted by a call. The voice belongs to a synthetic customer generated from that future. Then the screen says the thing the product is really about: this customer does not exist yet.

Who is this for? Anyone who is about to ship a policy, a price, or an assistant into a living customer system and would rather meet the damage in a rehearsal than in production. Analysts, operators, and product people who are used to dashboards after the fact — Aftermath is the rehearsal before the fact.

The input is a decision plus a seeded world. The output is not a single score. It is a month of lives, a named failure pattern, and optional milder universes sitting beside the one you proposed.

A useful picture is a crash-test dummy. The dummy is not a person. It still tells you what a car does to a body. Aftermath's customers are not real. They still tell you what a decision does to a relationship: who complains, who gets stuck with a bad assistant, who is offered a discount that finance will not honour, who leaves.

None of this replaces talking to actual users. It is a rehearsal so that the first time a chain reaction happens, it happens in a room where you can still change the decision. That is the whole product: meet the customer your policy has not created yet, before production creates them for you.
