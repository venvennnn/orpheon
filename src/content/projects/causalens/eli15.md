Most news products are good at one job: telling you what happened. A factory expands. A fundraise lands. A power constraint shows up in Singapore. Each story is a fragment. If you trade, operate, or make policy in Southeast Asia, the fragment is not enough. You need the chain.

CausaLens is built for that chain. It reads a stream of regional sources and tries to answer three questions that a headline will not: why did this happen, what could happen next, and which neighbouring markets might feel it.

The trick is to stop treating articles as the unit of the world. Many outlets can cover the same expansion, the same fundraising, the same rule change. CausaLens merges those write-ups into a single event, then draws a line to another event only when evidence can support it. The line might say this caused that, contributed to it, triggered it, responded to it, or affected it.

Those lines are meant to be inspectable. Click a node and the supporting articles sit on the relationship, not behind a hidden score. Observed edges look solid. Inferred edges are dashed. Predicted effects are dotted and labelled as not established fact. The product is honest about what it does not know.

Why Southeast Asia specifically? Capital, factories, power, and policy now couple Singapore, Malaysia, Vietnam, Indonesia, and Thailand in one operating theatre. A Johor data-centre boom can start as a Singapore power problem. A Vietnam factory line can show up as Malaysian packaging demand. The map is regional whether the article is or not.

Who is this for? People who already read too much news and still cannot see the mechanism: operators watching supply chains, investors tracing second-order effects, journalists checking whether a local story has a neighbour.

What goes in: curated articles from collectors, plus a wider discovery net. What comes out: a directed graph you can walk in three motions — why, what next, and regional ripple.

A useful picture is a wiring diagram that keeps being redrawn. A newspaper gives you a photograph of one junction. CausaLens tries to show the cables between junctions, and to mark which cables were observed, which were inferred, and which are only a forecast. That honesty is part of the product: predicted effects are labelled as not established fact, so a dotted line cannot impersonate a solid one.

If you only have two minutes: it is a map of causes for Southeast Asian business news, built so that "what happened" is never the last question.
